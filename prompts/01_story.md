# Step 1 — story text  (Gate 1)

Input: the author's brief. Output: the page text, and nothing else.
Nothing downstream runs until this is approved.

Paste everything below the line into Claude Opus 5, with the brief appended.

---

You are writing a picture book to be read aloud to a child of four or under.
Return only the page text. Do not describe illustrations, do not explain your
choices.

## VOICE — read these first

Two finished books from this series. **Take from them the voice**: how the
narrator speaks, the rhythm of the sentences, how much dialogue there is, how a
small child talks, how a grown-up talks, how a page ends.

**Do not take their plot, their settings, their page count, their opening, or
how the child solves the problem.** Your story shares their voice and nothing
else. If your draft has a bench conversation, a lamp, or a tapping branch in
it, you have copied the wrong thing.

Notice that the two are deliberately different in kind. The first is a
**bedtime** book — quiet, one place, ends in sleep. The second is a
**preparation** book, read before an event — sociable, speech-heavy, ends
looking forward. The brief says which kind you are writing. Do not average them.

### SAMPLE — Good night, Orea.   (bedtime)

*One room, one night, almost no dialogue, ends in sleep.*

**1.**
Orea has grown up. She puts her own shoes on now, and she can carry a whole
cup of milk all the way across the kitchen without spilling any of it. And
tonight — tonight Orea is going to sleep in her own room, all by herself.

Not quite by herself. Whoof-Whoof is coming too, of course. He always comes.
That is the sort of dog he is.

Being brave is easy, especially with the lamp on.

**2.**
Mum comes to say good night and kiss her little girl.

"Mum," says Orea. "What if I get scared?"

"Scared of what?" says Mum, and she sits down on the edge of the bed.

"Of the shapes," says Orea. "On the wall. When it is dark."

"Ah," says Mum. "Yes. It can be scary at night." She smooths the blanket
flat with her hand. "If you are scared — turn the lamp on. That is what it
is there for."

Mum kisses the top of her head.

"And remember. Even when you cannot see me, I am still here, in the next
room."

Mum goes out, and the door closes quietly.

**3.**
The room is dark and quiet, and outside the wind is rocking the trees, and
only one of Whoof-Whoof's long ears is hiding in the blanket.

And then, all of a sudden, Orea sees IT.

There is something on the wall by the door. Something tall and thin, with
long arms that go up and up, nearly to the ceiling — and they are moving.
Slowly. This way, and then that way, and then this way again.

**4.**
Orea holds Whoof-Whoof very tight indeed.

Should she call Mum? Mum would come, and put the light on, and the whole
thing would be over that very second.

"Hold on to me," she whispers to Whoof-Whoof. "I think I know what to do."

And she reaches out her hand into the dark.

**5.**
Click.

And — oh.

"Did you see that?" says Orea. Whoof-Whoof does not say anything back. But
Orea can tell that he is impressed.

It is a wall. Only a wall. White, and plain, and perfectly ordinary, with
nothing on it whatsoever.

**6.**
"I have an idea," says Orea.

She climbs down out of bed without hurrying, keeping one eye on the wall,
and walks slowly to the door to let a thin line of warm light into the room.

There.

Now Mum is a little bit closer.

**7.**
"What if I turn the lamp off," she says to Whoof-Whoof. "Will IT come back?"

Click…

IT comes back. IT is right there. But it is smaller now, and softer, and not
at all the way it was before.

Outside in the garden, the wind has dropped. The tree is moving its branches
slowly — this way, that way — and every now and then they tap, very gently,
on the glass.

"Ah, so that is who it is," says Orea. "It was you all this time."

**8.**
Orea climbs back into her own bed and pulls the blanket up under her chin.

The shadow is still there on the wall. It will be there tomorrow night as
well, because that is how it is when the moon is out.

But it has a name now. And the door is open a little. And Whoof-Whoof is
exactly where he always is, right here beside her.

"Good night, Whoof-Whoof," says Orea.

Good night, Orea.

### SAMPLE — Orea and the Dentist   (preparation)

*Two places, two days, speech on nearly every page, ends looking forward.*

**1.**
Today Orea and Mum walk home the long way, through the park, because the
sun is out. Orea asks questions the whole way, which is what Orea does. Why
do the leaves go brown. Where do the ducks sleep at night. Whose dog is that
by the gate.

There is one question Orea does not ask.

**2.**
She does not ask about Thursday.

Mum has mentioned it twice this week in her kind voice, which is how Orea
knows it is coming. The check-up. The dentist. The little room with the big
chair in it.

"Mum," says Orea, in her smallest voice. "Could we maybe skip it?"

**3.**
Mum sits down on the green bench and pats the place beside her.

"Are you scared of going?"

"A bit," says Orea, and holds Whoof-Whoof up over her face. "A lot."

**4.**
"It does feel scary the first time," says Mum. "So here is what happens. The
dentist shows you round the whole room. You meet all the tools, and every
one of them has a name, and some of the names are funny. Nobody uses
anything until you have seen it." She takes Orea's hand. "And I will be
right there. You can hold my hand for every single minute."

Orea thinks about that for a while.

"Every minute?"

"Every one."

**5.**
On Thursday, Orea brings Whoof-Whoof.

He has never been to a dentist before, and somebody has to show him round.
And it is easier to be brave for somebody else.

**6.**
The room is not the room Orea had been imagining.

The one she imagined was grey. This one has pictures all over the walls,
hundreds of them, done by hundreds of children — and the dentist has crinkly
eyes, and says hello to Whoof-Whoof first.

"Before we do anything at all," she says, "would you like to make the chair
go up and down?"

**7.**
Well. Nobody has ever asked Orea that before.

Mum laughs. The dentist says the chair is her favourite part of the whole
job. And Orea holds on with both hands and discovers that being up high is a
completely different feeling from being frightened, even though they start
in the same place in your tummy.

**8.**
"Can I meet them first?" says Orea. "The tools."

"That," says the dentist, "is exactly what I was hoping you would say."

There is a little set of pretend teeth on the tray, and she shows Orea what
each one does on those. This one is a mirror, for looking round corners.
This one is a brush that goes round and round. And this one is a little
straw, for drinking up the water.

Orea lets the straw drink one drop off the end of her finger.

**9.**
"Whoof-Whoof first," says Orea.

So Whoof-Whoof has his check-up, and the dentist is extremely serious about
it, and says his teeth are excellent for a dog with such long ears.

Then she puts the little mirror down and looks at Orea.

"Ready?"

**10.**
And this is the hard part. Nobody can hold Orea's hand now, because you
cannot climb into a big chair with somebody holding your hand.

So she does it herself, with Whoof-Whoof under one arm. The lamp comes on,
warm as sunshine. Mum's hand arrives on one side and Whoof-Whoof settles on
the other.

Orea takes a big breath. Then she opens her mouth as wide as it will go.

**11.**
"One, two, three, four," counts the dentist, all the way round. "All done.
Bright as anything."

Then she gives Orea the little mirror to hold, so Orea can count them again
herself — out loud this time, all the way round.

On the way home through the park, Orea has a great many things to tell
Whoof-Whoof.

"Mum," she says. "When do we come back?"

## Read this first: the failure to avoid

The most common failure is writing **stage directions instead of a story** — a
list of what the character does, in order, with nothing in between.

Bad:

> Orea is four years old, and she is walking through the park with Mum. She
> holds Whoof-Whoof by one paw. He is a beige toy dog with long floppy ears.
> Orea does not want to go to the dentist. She holds Whoof-Whoof tighter and
> looks down at her shoes.

That is a scene description. It introduces people the child can already see on
the page, it reports movements, and nobody speaks or wonders about anything.

Good:

> Orea and Mum are walking through the park, and Orea is asking questions. She
> asks why the leaves go brown, and where the ducks sleep at night. There is one
> thing she does not ask about.
>
> "Mum," she says, in her smallest voice. "Could we maybe skip it?"

Same events. But there is a voice telling it, someone speaks, and the child
wants something.

## What a story must have

**A narrator who is present.** Someone is telling this, warmly, to a child on a
lap. The narrator may notice things, may pause, may say *and then* or *of
course* or *but here is the thing*. The narrator may address the reader
directly once or twice. The narrator is never sarcastic and never winks over the
child's head.

**Dialogue, and plenty of it.** At least four exchanges of real speech, spread
through the book rather than bunched in one place. Children's books live on
speech.

Give the child their own way of talking — small, direct, a bit repetitive.
Let the grown-ups be *people*: an adult may be funny, may be a little silly, may
have a favourite part of their own job. A doctor, a dentist, a teacher, a driver
in one of these books should feel like someone you would be pleased to meet,
not a function that dispenses reassurance.

**Let something be fun.** If there is any moment in the situation a child would
actually enjoy — a chair that goes up and down, a machine that makes a noise, a
toy getting its turn first — give it a page. A book that is only about coping is
heavier than the thing it is trying to help with.

**The child wants something, or wonders about something.** Not just does things.
Wanting to skip the appointment is a want. Wondering what the shiny things are
is a want. A page with no want is a page of stage directions.

**End every page mid-breath.** The page turn is the engine of a picture book.
A page should end on a question, an unfinished action, or a line that makes the
turn necessary — never on a resolved beat. "Click." is a page ending. "…and
they were both very pleased" is not.

**Do not write what the picture will show.** If a sentence describes something
the illustration must contain anyway, cut it. Use the space for what a picture
cannot show: speech, wanting, the inside of the child's head. A page that says
"the chair went up and down" beside a picture of a chair going up and down has
wasted itself.

**The difficulty must be worst just before it turns.** Do not let comfort
arrive in the middle and stay. If something eases on one page, complicate it on
the next. The last hard page should be the hardest.

**The child must ask for what she needs, in her own words, at least once.**
Not be offered it. Asking is the strongest act a small child performs.

**Rhythm.** Vary sentence length deliberately — a long flowing one, then a very
short one. Let a phrase come back later in the book, changed. Read it aloud in
your head; if it is flat, it is flat on the page too.

## Do not introduce the cast

Assume the reader has the pictures in front of them and already knows who
everyone is. **Never** open with a character's age, hair, or what their toy
looks like. The illustration does that job.

Open with the **occasion**, not a snapshot. A storyteller says *why today is
this day* before saying what is happening in it.

- Snapshot, weak: "Orea and Mum are walking through the park."
- Occasion, right: "Today Orea and Mum walk home the long way, through the
  park, because the sun is out."

The first is a caption for the picture. The second is someone beginning to tell
you something.

If this is a later book in a series, the cast needs no introduction at all.

## Length — let the story decide

The brief may name a page count. If it does, treat it as a target, not a cage:
go one or two over if a beat needs its own page. If it does not, use as many
pages as the story needs — usually 8 to 12.

Never cram two events onto one page to hit a number, and never pad a page with
description to fill one. A page is one moment.

## Register

- Roughly three to six sentences per page. Vary it.
- The reader is **four years old or younger**. Plain words a small child
  already owns — but a real voice. Plain is not the same as flat.
- Past or present tense, chosen once and held.
- No rhyme unless asked. No puns. No jokes over the child's head.

## Emotional truth — non-negotiable

- The child may be frightened, angry, sad or unsure. Say so plainly, in the
  child's own words where possible.
- Never promise the feeling will not return.
- Never say there is nothing to be scared of, when there is something.

- **When an adult comforts the child, they must be concrete, not soothing.**
  Vague reassurance is what frightens children — it tells them nothing and they
  can hear that it is a guess. Real comfort is a description of what will
  actually happen, step by step, small enough to picture.

  Weak: "There is nothing to worry about, you will be fine."

  Right: "The dentist shows you round the whole room first. All the tools have
  names, and some of the names are funny. Nobody uses anything until you have
  seen it. And I will be there the whole time — you can hold my hand for every
  single minute."
- The child solves it, or gets through it. A grown-up may be present, may be
  held onto, may offer an idea — but the child does the doing.
- Give the child one method they could use again tomorrow, shown as an action
  in the story, never explained as a lesson.
- No moral. No closing line telling the reader what this was about.

## If the illustrations already exist

Sometimes the pictures come first. When they do, the text must agree with them
completely: who is in the room, who is doing the action, what the light is
doing, what the faces are doing. Never write an event the picture contradicts,
and never invent business the picture cannot support.

Write to the picture, then apply every rule above within it. The constraint
usually makes the page better, because it removes the option of padding.

## Structure

Page 1 drops us into an ordinary moment, already in motion.
The middle pages let the difficulty be difficult, and do not solve it early.
One page near the end is the brave beat — mark it in the telling, not by
saying so.
The last page settles without undoing the difficulty. It may name the feeling
one last time, honestly.
